## Ingredients

- [ ] 1¾ cups all-purpose flour
- [ ] 1 cup neutral oil, (vegetable, canola, avocado)
- [ ] 3 cups onion, small dice
- [ ] 2 cups celery, small dice
- [ ] 2 cups poblano peppers, small dice
- [ ] 2 cups okra, sliced ¼–½ inch thick
- [ ] 2 tbsp garlic, minced, about 6–8 cloves
- [ ] 2 lb bone-in, skin-on chicken thighs, about 2 lbs
- [ ] 13 oz andouille sausage, quartered and sliced, about 13 oz
- [ ] 6½ cups chicken stock
- [ ] 2 tbsp Better Than Bouillon chicken base
- [ ] 3 packets packets unflavoured gelatin
- [ ] 1 tbsp paprika
- [ ] 1 tsp cayenne pepper, adjust to taste
- [ ] 1 tsp black pepper
- [ ] 2 tsp Louisiana hot sauce, or your preferred Louisiana-style hot sauce
- [ ] salt to taste

### FOR SERVING

- [ ] cooked white rice, (300g long grain rice, 350g water, 5g salt)
- [ ] sliced scallions , for garnish

## Instructions

1. Season chicken thighs with salt on both sides. Let it sit for 10-30 minutes.
2. In a nonstick skillet over medium-high, sear diced andouille until browned. Remove.
3. In the same pan, brown chicken thighs over medium heat on both sides, about 5 minutes per side. Remove and set aside.
4. Medium dice onion, celery, and poblanos. Slice the okra into 1 inch rounds. Mince the garlic.
5. To make the roux, in a large dutch oven over medium heat, combine 225g neutral oil and 225g all-purpose flour. Stir constantly, reducing to medium-low as color develops. Cook 45 minutes until the colour becomes a deep chocolate brown *(see photos and a more detailed description of the roux process above)*
6. Add onion, celery, & poblanos to the roux. Toast 5-6 minutes. At this point, the roux should begin to stick to the bottom of the pot. Add garlic and stir for another minute.
7. While veggies toast, make a fortified stock by whisking together stock, Better than Bouillon, and gelatin. Gradually add it to the toasted roux and vegetables pot about 2c at a time, stirring constantly and allowing liquid to come to a simmer before adding more liquid.
8. Dice the seared chicken thighs into 1" cubes, then add to the pot with the seared andouille, any resting juices from the meats and the okra. Bring back to a gentle simmer and cook for about 25 minutes. Stirring every 5 minutes or so.
9. 15 minutes in, come back and skim off the okra scum and any roux oil that has risen to the surface.
10. Continue to cook until the chicken is just starting to be shreddable, and the trinity veg/okra are tender but not mushy.
11. Once the chicken is tender, add cayenne, paprika, black pepper, and hot sauce. Taste and adjust the salt if needed.
12. Serve in bowls with a scoop of rice on top. Garnish with scallions.

## Notes

- **Storage and Make-Ahead** Gumbo keeps well in the fridge for 5-6 days and actually tastes MUCH MUCH better the next day as the flavours continue to meld. It’s actually kind of crazy how good it is on day 2 or even after just a 60 minutes rest on the stovetop day-of. To reheat, warm it gently on the stovetop over medium-low heat, adding a splash of stock or water if it’s thickened up too much overnight. You can also freeze gumbo in airtight containers for up to 3 months. Thaw overnight in the fridge before reheating.
- **Take the Roux Deep –** Your target is a colour just darker than melted milk chocolate, but not as dark as dark chocolate. I like to take it to where I’m just starting to get a little scared of it. As it darkens, lower the heat to avoid burning. A darker roux brings deeper, more interesting flavour so push it a little further than you might be comfortable with. 
- **Fortified Stock –** Boxed stock tastes weak and has no body. Whisking in unflavoured gelatin and chicken bouillon paste simulates the richness and mouthfeel of a stock that’s been on the stove for hours. I use this move all the time to approximate slow simmered home made stock. 
- **Searing Meats in a Separate Pan –** Browning the chicken and andouille in a nonstick lets you sear while the roux cooks (if you can manage both), which makes the process smoother. If you did it in the Dutch oven, you’d need to clean it out before starting the roux so the browned bits don’t burn. Washing a heavy dutch oven 2 times in one day is annoying.  
- **Toasting the vegetables directly in the roux –** Instead of sautéing the vegetables separately, add them straight into the hot roux. This “arrests” the roux, stopping it from getting darker, and gives the vegetables a deeper, almost fried-in-oil flavour. That’s much more delicious than just simmering them in the liquid until tender. 
- **Add stock gradually, not all at once –** Pouring in a cup at a time hydrates the flour in the roux more evenly, so it incorporates smoothly without clumping. It also heats up the liquid faster, which means you’re at a simmer sooner.
- **Use an ice cream scoop for the rice –** If you have it, a round scoop of white rice dropped right into the center of the bowl gives you a classic gumbo presentation and a good rice to gravy ratio in every bite.

>[!qna]- Can I use butter instead of oil for the roux?
>HARD NO. We’re cooking this roux at a high temp for a LONG time, and  and the milk solids in butter will burn well before you reach the colour you need. Stick with a neutral oil like peanut.

>[!qna]- Is there a more hands-off way to make The roux?
>Yes. You can make an oven roux by combining the oil and flour in an oven-safe pan and baking it at 400°F, stirring every 15 minutes until it reaches the right colour. It takes 2–3 times longer than the stovetop method, so plan ahead. I’d recommend doing it the day before. You can also use a store-bought gumbo base like Tony Chachere’s, which can be a roughly 1:1 substitute for the roux and will save you about 45 minutes.  In my opinion nothing is a substitute for real homemade roux though.

>[!qna]- Can You make Gumbo gluten-free?
>You can, but keep a close eye on it. Gluten-free flour browns differently than regular AP flour, and the thickening power can vary. Be prepared to adjust at the end—a tiny bit of filé powder or a small cornstarch slurry can help if the consistency is off.

>[!qna]- I don’t like Okra. Is there a Substitute?
>Totally fine to skip it. But gumbo relies on at least two thickening agents (roux, okra, or filé powder) for proper body. If you leave out the okra, make sure you add filé powder at the end—off the heat—so the gumbo still thickens up the way it should.

>[!qna]- Can You Use pre-made Cajun seasoning For Gumbo?
>Most commercial Cajun blends are either too spicy, not spicy enough, or way too salty. None of them have anything special I don’t already have on my shelf. By using cayenne, paprika, and black pepper separately, you get complete control over your heat level. And chances are, you already own all three.
